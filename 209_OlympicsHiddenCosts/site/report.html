Olympics — The Hidden Costs

By Floriane Leynaud, Hope Tadsen, Bernard Julve — DATASCI 209, Summer 2026

⌄

Every four years, a new city takes on the Olympic bill. Since 1976, fourteen cities across five continents have played host — and each has walked away with a very different fiscal and social footprint.

The case for hosting is a familiar one: the International Olympic Committee (IOC) points to economic windfalls and a surge of national pride. This report puts those promises to the test, tracing the hidden costs the Games leave behind for host cities and their residents:

  1. The debt burden for cities
  2. The cost vs revenue generated by the organizing committee
  3. The GDP outcome in host countries
  4. The CO₂ emissions and impact on happiness

1 — Host cities carry the debt for decades

The Olympic bill doesn't disappear when the closing ceremony ends. Every host since 1976 is plotted below — each circle sized by its sports-related cost (venues and operations, in 2022 US$) and coloured by how long the host carried the debt. Montreal (1976) needed 30 years to pay off its stadium; Athens' (2004) venues still sit on the public books. Use the filter to compare Summer and Winter Games.

🖱️ Hover over any city for its cost, overrun and debt outcome.

* The circle's Cost is the sports-related figure (venues and operations); the all-in Games bill — transport, security and urban infrastructure — is considerably larger. We use it because it is defined consistently across every Games (Flyvbjerg et al., Oxford), so hosts decades apart are directly comparable; broader "total cost" numbers are reported inconsistently and often unavailable.

2 — The cost of hosting is higher than the revenues generated by the organizing committee

Did the Games pay for themselves? A look at what each edition cost against the revenue it brought in. The chart on the left is called a streamgraph where area represents total values. A streamgraph's usefulness is in it's ability to show the magnitude of change over time. It appears that revenues and costs of hosting an Olympic event has an upward trend over the past 50 years.

Note that all dollar amounts have been normalized to USD 2018. Also note that for years with multiple Olympic events, the totals are cumulative for the entire year covering both events.

🖱️ Hover your mouse over any year on the left chart to move the dashed line and to update the Financial Breakdown on the right chart.

3 — Hosting an Olympic Event is not linked to GDP increasing

Does hosting spend track with a country's economic growth? Try comparing the cost of the Games against host-nation GDP trends over the past 50 years. We fail to see a strong correlation either way, with some years showing increased GDP growth after an Olympic Year, others showing a decline, and still others remaining steady.

From GDP to Generosity this data set lets us explore how the Olympics impacted happiness accross countries that hosted and those who did not host. To visualized this we chose to use the World Happiness Report ranking, based on self-reported wellbeing.

Takeaways

  1. Hosting the Olympics saddles host communities with enormous costs.
  2. Those costs are routinely underestimated — overruns run into the billions.
  3. And it is the public, not the IOC, that carries the debt, often for decades.
  4. GDP growth and hosting appear largely uncorrelated — Summer Games show slight upticks, Winter Games slight downticks, but not consistently.
  5. Fifty years ago the cost–revenue gap was wider, though the absolute figures were smaller.
  6. Hosting shows no significant effect on emissions per capita, nor on the host country's happiness.
